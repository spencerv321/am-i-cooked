# Company Cooked — Validation Test Results

Five companies, five different profiles. Read these critically. 
Where is the analysis sharp? Where is it bullshit? Where does the framework break?

## Test 1: PDQ (Private — Spencer knows this company intimately)

**Overall Score: 49 — MEDIUM**

**Hot Take:** "Your customers aren't leaving — they just won't need half your sales team to be convinced to stay."

**Summary:** PDQ occupies an interesting middle ground: its core product (device management, software deployment for IT admins) solves a real operational problem that doesn't go away just because AI exists. Schools and enterprises still need to manage thousands of endpoints. But the way PDQ builds, sells, and supports that product is about to change radically — AI compresses the engineering effort to build competitors, thins out the go-to-market teams, and pressures the per-seat pricing model that funds it all.

| Dimension | Score | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Product Disruption | 38 | Core product still needed |
| Headcount Vulnerability | 61 | AI will thin the ranks |
| Moat Erosion | 55 | Switching costs holding, but eroding |
| Business Model Risk | 58 | Per-seat pressure building |
| Market Viability | 32 | EdTech IT isn't going anywhere |

**Product Disruption (38):** Device management and software deployment require deep OS-level integration, agent-based architecture, and enterprise reliability that AI can't just conjure from a prompt. An AI agent can't push a Windows update to 10,000 school district machines — you need actual infrastructure for that. The product isn't at risk of being "replaced by a chatbot." What IS at risk: the simpler parts of the product (inventory reporting, basic troubleshooting, patch management logic) could be handled by AI-native tools that are easier to set up and cheaper to run.

**Headcount Vulnerability (61):** This is where PDQ feels it. The sales motion — SDRs qualifying leads, AEs running demos, CSMs managing renewals — is highly exposed to AI automation. Marketing content, support ticket resolution, and internal documentation can all be AI-assisted or AI-replaced. Engineering gets more productive with AI coding tools, meaning the same output from fewer people. A company PDQ's size probably needs 25-35% fewer people in 3 years to maintain the same output, with the deepest cuts in go-to-market and support functions.

**Moat Erosion (55):** PDQ's moats are real but not invincible. Switching costs in enterprise IT are genuinely high — school districts don't rip out device management infrastructure casually. Existing customer relationships and institutional knowledge matter. But the barrier to *building* a PDQ competitor has dropped dramatically. What used to take a 50-person engineering team and 3 years of development can now be prototyped by a small team with AI in months. The moat isn't the product anymore — it's the customer relationships and installed base.

**Business Model Risk (58):** Per-seat or per-device SaaS pricing is under structural pressure across the industry. If AI agents can manage devices and deployments with less human oversight, the "value per seat" argument weakens. PDQ won't face an overnight pricing crisis, but over 2-4 years, new entrants will undercut on price because they need fewer humans to build and support the product. The question is whether PDQ can transition to value-based or outcome-based pricing before the pricing pressure arrives.

**Market Viability (32):** The good news: schools still buy computers, employees still get laptops, IT departments still need to manage them. The underlying market for device management isn't shrinking — if anything, the proliferation of devices increases it. EdTech IT spending is stable to growing. The market isn't the problem; the margin structure within the market is.

**What Would Kill It:** An AI-native device management platform built by a 10-person startup that's 80% as good at 30% of the price, targeting the same education vertical with a self-serve onboarding experience that doesn't require a sales team.

**What Keeps It Alive:** Deeply embedded in thousands of school districts with multi-year contracts, high switching costs, and IT admins who trust the product. Enterprise IT buyers are the most risk-averse buyers on earth — they don't switch tools because something cheaper appeared.

**AI Adaptation Signals:** Would need to check PDQ's recent product announcements, job postings, and conference presentations for AI integration signals. Companies in this space that are adding AI-powered troubleshooting, predictive patch management, and natural language query interfaces are adapting. Those that aren't are running out the clock on their switching cost moat.

**Timeline:** 2-4 years before meaningful revenue or headcount impact.

## Test 2: Salesforce (CRM — Mega-cap public)

**Overall Score: 62 — MEDIUM**

**Hot Take:** "Salesforce spent $27 billion on Slack and Tableau to build a moat. AI just built a bridge over it for $20/month."

**Summary:** Salesforce is the most interesting case study in AI disruption because it's simultaneously the most threatened and the most resourced to adapt. The core CRM product — tracking contacts, logging activities, managing pipelines — is trivially replicable by AI. But Salesforce isn't really a CRM company anymore; it's an enterprise data platform with tentacles in every department. The question isn't whether AI can replace Salesforce's features. It's whether Salesforce's installed base and ecosystem lock-in can survive long enough for the company to reinvent itself as an AI platform. It's a race between disruption speed and adaptation speed.

| Dimension | Score | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Product Disruption | 72 | Core CRM is highly replicable |
| Headcount Vulnerability | 68 | Bloated and exposed |
| Moat Erosion | 48 | Ecosystem is holding — for now |
| Business Model Risk | 75 | Per-seat pricing is the bullseye |
| Market Viability | 40 | CRM need persists, form factor changes |

**Product Disruption (72):** The core CRM functionality — contact management, deal tracking, activity logging, pipeline reporting — can be replicated by an AI agent that reads your email, listens to your calls, and maintains the CRM autonomously. The manual data entry that Salesforce depends on (and that reps hate) is exactly the kind of tedious, structured work AI eliminates. Salesforce's more complex products (Marketing Cloud, Service Cloud, Commerce Cloud, Tableau) have more defensibility because they involve complex integrations and workflow orchestration. But the flagship product that carries the brand? Highly exposed.

**Headcount Vulnerability (68):** Salesforce has ~73,000 employees. That number is indefensible at current revenue levels in an AI world. They've already done layoffs (10% in 2023). AI will continue to compress the engineering org (fewer people to build features), the support org (AI-first support), and critically the massive sales org — Salesforce's own sales motion (SDR → AE → SE → CSM) is exactly the kind of multi-touch enterprise sales process that AI is automating. The irony of a CRM company having its own sales team disrupted by AI is not lost on anyone.

**Moat Erosion (48):** Here's where Salesforce is stronger than people think. The ecosystem moat is enormous — thousands of ISV partners, millions of Salesforce admins and developers whose careers depend on the platform, petabytes of customer data locked in Salesforce instances, and deep integrations with every other enterprise tool. Ripping out Salesforce is a multi-year, multi-million dollar project for any large enterprise. This is the moat that buys time. But it's an eroding moat: every year, migration tools get better, AI makes integration easier, and the next generation of buyers has less loyalty to the Salesforce ecosystem.

**Business Model Risk (75):** Per-seat pricing is ground zero for AI disruption. If AI agents handle 40% of what a sales rep does, companies need fewer seats. If AI handles CRM data entry automatically, the "admin" seats go away. Salesforce sees this coming — they launched Agentforce and are trying to pivot to consumption/outcome-based pricing. But pivoting the revenue model of a $35B/year company without tanking revenue is like changing the engine on a plane mid-flight. The transition risk is enormous.

**Market Viability (40):** Companies will always need to manage customer relationships. The need for CRM doesn't disappear. What changes is the *form factor* — instead of a human logging data into a CRM, an AI agent manages the relationship data autonomously. The market shifts from "software that helps humans manage relationships" to "AI that manages relationships with human oversight." Salesforce can ride this transition if it moves fast enough. The market isn't dying; it's transforming.

**What Would Kill It:** An AI-native "anti-CRM" that requires zero manual data entry — it reads email, listens to calls, watches calendar, and maintains a perfect relationship graph automatically. Sold at 20% of Salesforce's per-seat price because there are no "seats" — just an AI agent per company. If this gets good enough for mid-market, Salesforce's growth engine stalls.

**What Keeps It Alive:** The installed base. Enterprise switching costs. The ecosystem of partners and admins. And $35B in annual revenue that funds a massive AI R&D budget. Salesforce has more resources to adapt than almost any other company on this list — the question is whether the organizational culture can move fast enough.

**AI Adaptation Signals:** Agentforce launch, Einstein AI features, aggressive marketing around "AI CRM." Salesforce is clearly aware of the threat and investing heavily. The question is execution — can a 73,000-person company with a complex product portfolio actually ship AI-native products fast enough to outrun a startup with 15 people and Claude Code?

**Timeline:** 12-24 months before meaningful revenue pressure; 3-5 years before existential questions.

## Test 3: Basecamp (Small Private Company — ~80 employees)

**Overall Score: 44 — MEDIUM**

**Hot Take:** "DHH has been arguing against bloated software for 20 years. AI just proved him right by making everything Basecamp does available for the cost of an API call."

**Summary:** Basecamp is a fascinating edge case because its philosophy (simple, opinionated, small team) is both its greatest defense and its greatest vulnerability. The product is deliberately simple — project management, team communication, to-dos. AI can replicate every feature trivially. But Basecamp has never competed on features; it competes on opinion, brand, and the anti-complexity movement. AI can replace the tool but it can't replace the worldview. The question is whether enough customers buy the worldview to sustain a business when the tool itself is commoditized.

| Dimension | Score | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Product Disruption | 71 | Features are trivially replicable |
| Headcount Vulnerability | 25 | Already lean, mostly safe |
| Moat Erosion | 52 | Brand moat is real but thin |
| Business Model Risk | 45 | Flat pricing helps, actually |
| Market Viability | 38 | PM tools persist but evolve |

**Product Disruption (71):** Let's be blunt: an AI agent with Claude can build a Basecamp-quality project management tool in a weekend. The features — to-do lists, message boards, file sharing, scheduling, check-ins — are simple by design. That was always the point, and it's now the vulnerability. Any company evaluating Basecamp vs. a custom AI-built internal tool will do the math and realize the custom tool is nearly free.

**Headcount Vulnerability (25):** This is where Basecamp's philosophy saves it. They've been a ~80-person company by choice for years. There's not much to cut. They don't have a bloated sales org, a massive support team, or an army of engineers. The humans at Basecamp are mostly the leadership, core product team, and the brand voice (DHH and Jason Fried). AI makes these people more productive but doesn't make them redundant because the company was already operating lean.

**Moat Erosion (52):** Basecamp's moat was never technical — it's ideological. Customers choose Basecamp because they believe in the philosophy of simple, calm, anti-enterprise software. That brand loyalty is real but narrow. The switching costs are low (it's a simple tool, easy to migrate off), the data portability is good, and the network effects are minimal. The moat is a cult following, not a technical barrier.

**Business Model Risk (45):** Interestingly, Basecamp's flat-fee pricing ($349/month, unlimited users) is more AI-resilient than per-seat models. When AI reduces the number of humans at a company, per-seat revenue drops but Basecamp's revenue doesn't. The flat fee also makes it less of a target for cost-cutting than a tool that charges $15/user/month across 500 users. This is an accidental advantage.

**Market Viability (38):** Teams will still need to coordinate work. The market for "team collaboration" isn't disappearing. What changes is that AI handles more of the coordination automatically — scheduling, status updates, follow-ups, document organization. This shifts the market from "tools that help humans coordinate" to "AI that coordinates for humans." Simple tools like Basecamp may actually survive this better than complex ones like Asana or Monday.com because there's less to disrupt.

**What Would Kill It:** The brand voices (DHH, Jason Fried) losing relevance or the company losing its cultural moment. Basecamp survives on mindshare more than market share. If the anti-complexity movement fades or gets co-opted by AI-native tools that are even simpler, the brand moat evaporates.

**What Keeps It Alive:** A deliberately small business that doesn't need massive growth to survive. Profitable, no VC pressure, no public market expectations. Can sustain indefinitely on a loyal niche following. This is the most anti-fragile business structure on this list.

**AI Adaptation Signals:** HEY (their email product) shows willingness to build new products. DHH is vocal about AI and building with it. Small companies can pivot faster than large ones. The real question is whether they build AI into the product or resist it as part of the "calm" philosophy.

**Timeline:** 3-5 years. The small, profitable, niche model buys significant runway. No urgent existential threat.

## Test 4: Intuit (Already "Confirmed Hit" — Feb 4, 2026)

**Overall Score: 76 — WELL DONE**

**Hot Take:** "TurboTax's entire business model is that tax code is too complicated for normal people. Claude just read the entire tax code in 3 seconds."

**Summary:** Intuit is the clearest case of a company whose core value proposition is directly undermined by AI capabilities. TurboTax exists because tax preparation is complex. QuickBooks exists because bookkeeping is tedious. Mailchimp exists because marketing requires specialized knowledge. AI is rapidly making all three of these problems trivial. Intuit has the resources to adapt (they're a $150B+ company investing heavily in AI), but they're trying to defend a castle whose walls are made of "this is too hard for you to do yourself" — and AI just made it easy.

| Dimension | Score | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Product Disruption | 82 | AI does this natively now |
| Headcount Vulnerability | 72 | Massive org, heavily exposed |
| Moat Erosion | 65 | Regulatory complexity was the moat |
| Business Model Risk | 78 | Consumer willingness to pay collapsing |
| Market Viability | 55 | Tax and bookkeeping persist, but form changes |

**Product Disruption (82):** This is the highest product disruption score in this test set and it's warranted. TurboTax's core product is guided tax preparation — walking you through questions and filling out forms. Claude, GPT, and other LLMs can already do this conversationally, with access to the full tax code, for effectively zero marginal cost. QuickBooks' bookkeeping features — categorizing transactions, generating P&L statements, reconciling accounts — are precisely the kind of structured, rule-based tasks AI handles effortlessly. Credit Karma's financial recommendations are replaceable by any AI with access to your financial data. Every major Intuit product is squarely in AI's crosshairs.

**Headcount Vulnerability (72):** Intuit has ~18,000 employees. The seasonal TurboTax Live workforce (human tax preparers) is directly threatened — AI can answer the same questions better and faster. The engineering org can be compressed as AI coding tools mature. The massive marketing spend (Intuit spends over $1B/year on advertising) faces diminishing returns when consumers realize they can do their taxes by asking an AI for free. Support functions are highly automatable. Intuit probably needs 40-50% fewer people within 3-4 years.

**Moat Erosion (65):** Intuit's historical moat was that tax code is complicated and changes every year, creating a recurring knowledge gap that justifies paying for software annually. AI erases this moat because it can ingest the entire updated tax code instantly. The second moat — consumer data and financial history stored in QuickBooks/TurboTax — is more durable but not invincible. Data portability regulations and consumer willingness to switch when a free AI alternative appears will test this moat. The brand trust moat ("I've always used TurboTax") is real for older consumers but means nothing to Gen Z, who will ask Claude to do their taxes without a second thought.

**Business Model Risk (78):** Intuit charges $50-150 for TurboTax, $15-100/month for QuickBooks. These prices are defensible when the alternative is hiring an accountant. They're indefensible when the alternative is asking an AI. The consumer mental math shifts from "TurboTax vs. $400 CPA" to "TurboTax vs. free AI." Intuit's pricing power is collapsing from below. They can try to reposition as an "AI-powered financial platform," but that means competing with every AI assistant on the market, not just other tax software.

**Market Viability (55):** People will always need to file taxes and manage business finances. The market doesn't disappear. But the market for *paid software to do these things* might. When AI can do your taxes for free as a feature of your existing AI assistant, the standalone tax prep market contracts to complex cases only — high-net-worth, multi-entity, international. That's a fraction of Intuit's current market.

**What Would Kill It:** Apple, Google, or an AI company building tax preparation and basic bookkeeping into their core AI assistant as a free feature. If "Hey Siri, do my taxes" or "Hey Claude, reconcile my QuickBooks" becomes good enough for 70% of filers, Intuit's consumer business implodes.

**What Keeps It Alive:** Regulatory complexity (AI tax prep will face regulatory scrutiny and liability questions), the installed base of 100M+ customers, massive R&D budget to build AI features themselves, and the fact that enterprise QuickBooks (accountants using it for clients) has stickier switching costs than consumer TurboTax.

**AI Adaptation Signals:** Intuit has been investing heavily in AI — Intuit Assist, generative AI features across products, significant AI hiring. They see the threat clearly. The question is whether bolting AI onto existing products is enough, or whether the product paradigm itself is what's being disrupted. Adding AI features to TurboTax is like adding a touchscreen to a horse-drawn carriage — the improvement is real but the disruption is structural.

**Timeline:** 12-18 months. The market has already repriced this (INTU -34% in the Feb 4 selloff). The stock market sees it even if management is still in "we'll adapt" mode.

## Test 5: Walmart (Non-Tech — Retail/Logistics Giant)

**Overall Score: 28 — MEDIUM RARE**

**Hot Take:** "You can't download groceries. Walmart's 4,700 stores just became the world's most valuable AI-proof moat."

**Summary:** Walmart is what "AI-resistant" looks like at scale. The core business — moving physical goods from manufacturers to consumers through stores and logistics networks — is fundamentally physical work that AI can't displace. AI can optimize Walmart's operations enormously (and already is), but it can't replace the truck driver, the shelf stocker, the distribution center, or the physical store. Walmart isn't threatened by AI — it's *empowered* by it. The company that can deploy AI across 2.1 million employees and a $650B supply chain becomes more dominant, not less.

| Dimension | Score | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Product Disruption | 12 | AI can't stock shelves |
| Headcount Vulnerability | 35 | Some functions exposed, core isn't |
| Moat Erosion | 15 | Physical infrastructure is the ultimate moat |
| Business Model Risk | 18 | Retail model is AI-enhanced, not AI-threatened |
| Market Viability | 22 | People always need to eat |

**Product Disruption (12):** Walmart's "product" is the ability to get any of 150,000+ items into your hands, either in a store within 10 miles of 90% of Americans or delivered to your door within hours. AI can't do this. There is no AI-native competitor to "a building full of food near your house." E-commerce is a partial competitor (and Walmart is investing heavily there), but grocery — Walmart's largest category — remains stubbornly physical. AI will make Walmart's product better (smarter inventory, personalized recommendations, faster checkout), not obsolete.

**Headcount Vulnerability (35):** Walmart employs 2.1 million people in the US alone. The store-level jobs — stocking, cashiering, customer assistance — are partially automatable (self-checkout, shelf-scanning robots, automated fulfillment) but the timeline is measured in decades, not years, and social/political pressure will slow automation of this many jobs. Where AI does hit: corporate functions (finance, marketing, HR, legal), supply chain optimization (fewer planners needed), and customer service (chatbots replacing call center staff). Maybe 15-20% of corporate headcount is vulnerable, but that's a small fraction of total employees.

**Moat Erosion (15):** Walmart's moats are almost entirely AI-proof. The physical infrastructure (stores, distribution centers, truck fleet) can't be replicated by a startup with Claude Code. The supplier relationships and purchasing power (Walmart is the largest buyer for many manufacturers) aren't threatened by AI. The data moat (transaction data on 150M+ weekly customers) actually becomes MORE valuable with AI, not less — Walmart can use AI to optimize pricing, inventory, and personalization in ways competitors can't match.

**Business Model Risk (18):** Retail margins are already thin (~2.5% net), so there's not much fat to be disrupted. The business model — buy wholesale, sell retail, make it up on volume — is as old as commerce itself and AI doesn't change the fundamental economics. If anything, AI improves margins by optimizing inventory (less waste), automating back-office functions, and enabling more targeted marketing.

**Market Viability (22):** People will always need food, household goods, and basic necessities. The grocery market alone is $1.5T+ in the US. This market isn't going anywhere. The format may evolve (more delivery, more automation, more personalization), but the demand is permanent and growing with population.

**What Would Kill It:** Not AI. Walmart's existential risks are regulatory (antitrust, minimum wage), competitive (Amazon, Costco), and macroeconomic (consumer recession). AI is a tool Walmart uses, not a threat it faces.

**What Keeps It Alive:** Physical infrastructure that took 60+ years and hundreds of billions of dollars to build. 90% of Americans live within 10 miles of a Walmart. That's not a moat — it's a continent-wide fortress.

**AI Adaptation Signals:** Walmart is one of the most aggressive AI adopters in retail — AI-powered supply chain management, drone delivery testing, automated fulfillment centers, AI-assisted inventory management. They're using AI as a weapon, not defending against it.

**Timeline:** 5+ years before any meaningful AI-driven disruption to the core business. AI helps Walmart more than it hurts it for the foreseeable future.

## Framework Validation Notes

After running all five, here are the honest observations about what's working and what isn't:

**What's working:**
- The five dimensions DO produce meaningfully different scores per company — it's not just five ways of saying the same thing
- The framework handles both tech and non-tech companies (PDQ vs Walmart)
- The hot takes are specific and quotable, not generic
- The "what would kill it" / "what keeps it alive" pairing forces a balanced analysis
- Private vs public company analysis both feel substantive

**What might need work:**
- Headcount vulnerability for very small companies (Basecamp at 80 people) feels less meaningful — the dimension matters less when there's no bloat to cut
- The "AI adaptation signals" section is weakest for private companies where hiring data and product announcements aren't easily visible — may need the website scrape enrichment to fill this gap
- Market viability scoring feels the least differentiated — most markets "persist but evolve" which makes many scores cluster around 30-50
- Need to consider: should there be an "AI benefit" score too? Walmart is actually HELPED by AI. The current framework only measures risk, not opportunity. A company that's low-risk AND high-AI-benefit is in a fundamentally different position than one that's just low-risk.

**Edge cases to think about:**
- What about AI companies themselves? "Is OpenAI cooked?" produces a weird recursive result
- What about governments or nonprofits?
- What about companies that are primarily physical labor (construction, farming)?
- What about very new startups with no established product?
